# Nepal building typology

Buildings are resolved in this order: settlement context → geography → use → construction → family → floors → massing → roof → façade/openings → additions/utilities → age/maintenance → drawing variation.

## Contexts

- Urban: contemporary RCC houses, narrow plots, mixed-use shop-houses, ordinary historic Newar-inspired residences, rows, apartments and modest institutions.
- Semi-urban / peri-urban: road-facing RCC houses, RCC/corrugated hybrids, old/new extensions, workshops, commercial strips, godowns and modest lodges. Transition is its own condition, not an average of urban and rural.
- Rural: mountain, high-hill, mid-hill, inner-valley and Terai families using stone, brick, earth, timber, RCC and lightweight systems. Rural does not imply traditional or poorly maintained.

## Observable compatibility

- RCC frame: usually 2–5 urban floors or 1–4 semi-urban floors; flat roofs, balconies, tanks and incremental vertical work are plausible.
- Stone/earth/timber masonry: usually 1–2 floors; pitched slate, tile or corrugated roofs; compact openings and verandahs depend on geography.
- Terai lowland: low/wide massing, shade, verandahs and courtyard relationships are favored.
- Historic Valley masonry: narrow vertical frontage, brick surface rhythm, timber-window suggestions and tiled slopes; ordinary residences dominate.
- Hybrid buildings may combine old and new only through explicit incremental changes.

## Representation rules

Typology outranks decoration; massing outranks texture. Masonry is shown in selective patches. Plaster is mostly blank. Maintenance is independent from geography. Prayer flags, temples and decorative windows are never generic identity markers.
