# Style analysis

The references use economical, observational black-pen drawing: heads are dominant, irregular containers with large areas of unmarked paper. Features are built from very few strokes. Noses often carry the identity of a face and range from tiny hooks to long continuous bridges. Eyes vary independently in size, height, closure, and gaze. Ears are simple loops and frequently uneven.

Hair alternates between dense charcoal-like fill, parallel hatching, isolated tufts, and scribbled curls. Filled areas are used sparingly, making hair, glasses, moustaches, and caps visually anchor otherwise open faces. Lines wobble at a small scale, overlap occasionally, and close imperfectly. The work is naïve but deliberate rather than noisy.

Color remains subordinate to ink. A restricted, dusty palette—madder, indigo, marigold, moss, and clay—appears only on selected clothing, shawl or kurta cues, tiny accessories, and patterned Dhaka topis. Most faces remain monochrome, preventing cultural cues from becoming costume or stereotype.

Faces range from frontal to strong profile. Asymmetry, awkward spacing, exaggerated single features, and modest expressions produce individuality. Clothing is only suggested at the neck. The colored sheet introduces muted pencil blocks—clay, ochre, moss, slate—without shading or gloss. The generator follows this grammar without tracing any source face.

Cultural grounding is treated as a population-level property: contemporary grooming and clothing dominate, with occasional topis, shawls, scarves, jewelry, and winter caps. No facial structure is assigned an ethnicity, caste, religion, or region.
